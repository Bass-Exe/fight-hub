Kronk Protocol

Thomas "Hitman" Hearns & Anthony "The Magician" Sims Jr. · Kronk Gym, Detroit

The Kronk style was built in the basement of a Detroit rec center under trainer Emanuel Steward — gold and red trunks, a sweatbox gym, and one philosophy: control range with the jab, load every cross with knockout power, and never stand still long enough to get hit back. It turned Hearns into a five-division world champion, and it still shapes fighters today through the Kronk lineage, including modern light heavyweight Anthony Sims Jr.

Foot Placement Guide

KRONK — EXACT POSITIONING

Top-down view. Solid line = sole of your foot, heel to toe. Dashed = the direction it's aiming.

FRONT FOOT AIMS AT
OPPONENT'S LEAD SHOULDER
LEAD FOOT (L)
15–20°

Points slightly outward from the direct line to your opponent. Toes angle toward their lead (left) shoulder — not their nose, not their hip.

Heel is grounded but light. Ball of foot carries most pressure. This foot is your pivot point — the jab fires off it, the check hook pivots on it.

REAR FOOT (R)
40–45°

Angled outward behind the lead foot. Rear heel slightly outside the lead heel — not directly behind it. This creates the stable base for the Kronk stance.

Ball of foot loaded and ready. The rear foot is your power source for the cross and your push engine for forward movement.

Stance width: Side to side — roughly shoulder-width apart (about 14–18 in / 35–45 cm, depending on your build). Front to back — rear foot sits about 12–18 in behind the lead foot. Quick test: jump straight up and land naturally — wherever your feet come down is close to your correct width.
    COACHING CUES
  • "Lead toe at their lead shoulder." Not at their chest (too square), not at their hip (too wide). Imagine their left shoulder has a laser target on it — your lead toe points there.
  • "The stance is bladed but the lead toe still faces them." Your body turns sideways but your front foot doesn't swing with it. Keep that lead toe tracking toward the opponent even as your torso blades.
  • "Rear heel outside, not behind." If you line both heels up front-to-back on the same narrow line, you'll fall sideways. The rear heel needs that slight outward offset to create width.
  • "Pivot lives in the lead ball." Every pivot — check hook, angle change — rotates on the ball of the lead foot. If that ball isn't loaded, you have no pivot. Check it before every round.

Why Kronk footwork is hard: The tall, slightly bladed stance demands unusual hip flexibility to pivot cleanly and unusual calf/glute strength to stay on the balls of your feet for long rounds. Most people either collapse their weight back to the heel after a few minutes (killing their pivot speed) or lose the stance entirely and go flat. The fix is to train the foot and hip strength separately before expecting it to last in rounds.

BOUT 1 — RANGE WAR

The Pawing Jab & Stiff-Arm Control

Thomas "Hitman" Hearns · Kronk Gym, Detroit

Hearns' jab wasn't just a punch that scored points — it was a steering wheel. He used a long, pawing jab to find distance, a quicker flicker jab to freeze opponents and break their rhythm, and at times turned the same lead hand into a stiff-arm or collar-tie to control an opponent's head and balance. The jab decides everything that comes after it. Master this and the cross becomes a formality.

FEET

  • Stand slightly taller than your usual stance — Hearns used his height, you use your reach.
  • Lead foot points straight at the target. Back foot turned out ~45°, stance a touch wider than normal to support a long, extended jab.
  • Weight rides 55% on the back foot at range — this lets the front foot fire and recover without dragging your balance forward.
  • Push the jab off the ball of the back foot, never the heel.

HANDS

  • Lead hand floats slightly more extended than a normal guard — active and out in space, not glued to the cheek.
  • Rear hand stays tight at the cheekbone, elbow tucked to the ribs. This is your insurance while the lead hand works.
  • Pawing jab: extend slow, fingers loosely open like you're measuring distance, snap shut only the last few inches.
  • Flicker jab: same line, but fast and short — just enough to block vision and break timing.
  • Stiff-arm: extend the jab and instead of fully retracting, rest the glove on the target's shoulder line for half a second before pulling back.

BOUT 2 — THE ANGLE

Pivot Power & The Never-Repeat Combo

Anthony "The Magician" Sims Jr. · Kronk Lineage

Sims Jr. earned the nickname "The Magician" because opponents could never predict where the next shot was coming from. He generates knockout power from angles most fighters don't bother to find — pivoting off a jab instead of standing and trading, switching his combination mid-stream so the fourth punch never matches what the first three suggested. The lesson: power isn't just hip rotation, it's hip rotation from an angle your opponent didn't see you create.

FEET

  • Start square in your normal stance, but treat your lead foot as a pivot point, not just an anchor.
  • After your jab lands, pivot on the ball of the lead foot 30–45° — this rotates your whole body off the line your opponent is aiming at.
  • Rear foot follows the pivot, replanting to re-establish a full stance at the new angle.
  • Stay on the balls of both feet through the pivot — flat feet can't turn fast enough.

HANDS

  • Hands stay exactly where they are during the pivot. The angle change comes from your feet and hips — not from dropping the guard to "help" you turn.
  • Load power into the rear hand by coiling the rear hip and shoulder back slightly before you throw. Bigger coil, bigger snap.
  • After any 2–3 punch combo, the next punch should never match the pattern your opponent just saw. Threw jab-cross-hook? Don't follow with hook again — switch to an uppercut or a body shot from the new angle.

BOUT 3 — THE FINISH

The Chopping Cross & Full Fight Simulation

Hearns' Finisher + Sims' Angles, Combined

Hearns' jab created the opening — but the chopping right cross is what ended fights. Instead of a flat, straight cross, he threw his right hand on a slight downward angle, chopping over a guard that had been lowered or distracted by the jab. Combine that finishing power with Sims Jr.'s habit of creating an angle first, and you get the full Kronk formula: control range, create an angle, then chop the finishing shot down through the opening. Today you put the whole week together.

FEET

  • Same base stance — but as you throw the chopping cross, drive up and slightly forward off the back foot. Power comes from the floor up, not just the shoulder.
  • If you've pivoted first (Thursday's skill), the chopping cross now arrives from an angle your opponent isn't braced for. That's the whole point of stacking these two days.

HANDS

  • The chopping cross doesn't travel flat — it arcs slightly downward, like you're throwing it OVER something rather than through it.
  • Drop the rear shoulder slightly as you load, then drive up and over as you release. Your fist finishes higher than where the target's chin started — you've come down on top of it.
  • Lead hand stays up and tight to your own chin the instant you commit to the cross. This is the one punch where over-committing gets you caught.

LENNOX LEWIS · KRONK The Power Jab
▾

Most fighters treat the jab as a tool to measure distance. Steward took Lewis's ordinary jab and turned it into a weapon on its own — thrown with real force, not just to set up something else. Paired with a heavy straight right, it became one of the most damaging one-twos in the sport, capable of hurting an opponent before the right hand ever lands.

FEET

  • Same orthodox base, but plant slightly heavier on the lead foot as you throw — you're driving through the target, not just touching it.
  • Back foot stays grounded to anchor the rotation. This isn't a step-in jab, it's a loaded one from your existing range.

HANDS

  • Rotate the lead shoulder and hip into the punch the way you would a cross — it should carry the same intent as your power shots.
  • Snap the fist at full extension with real speed, then follow immediately with a straight right on the same line.

KRONK DEFENSE · TRICK The Check Hook
▾

When an opponent surges forward, most fighters retreat straight back — exactly what an aggressive fighter wants, since it keeps them in a straight line to chase. The check hook does the opposite: instead of retreating, you pivot off the lead foot and throw a hook as they step in, using their own momentum against them while you land on an angle they never saw coming.

FEET

  • As the opponent steps forward, pivot your lead foot roughly 90° rather than stepping backward.
  • Rear foot follows the pivot, landing you completely off their forward line — you end up almost beside them, not behind your old spot.

HANDS

  • Throw the lead hook AS you pivot, not before or after — the hook and the foot pivot happen on the same beat.
  • Keep the rear hand glued to your chin through the whole movement; you're moving past their power hand, not away from it.

GERALD McCLELLAN · KRONK The G-Man Hook
▾

Gerald McClellan's left hook was one of the most feared single punches the Kronk gym ever produced — a short, violent shot built from technique and timing as much as raw power, capable of changing a fight in an instant. The lesson isn't "swing harder," it's "transfer cleaner."

FEET

  • Same base stance — the hook starts in your back foot's toes, pivoting the same direction as the punch.
  • Weight transfers fully onto the front foot as the hook lands. Don't leave it split between both feet — that's where the power leaks out.

HANDS

  • Keep the hook short — elbow at roughly 90°, fist traveling in a tight arc, not a wide swing.
  • Power comes from hip and shoulder rotation snapping through; the arm is along for the ride. Rear hand stays at the chin the entire time.

KRONK FINISHER · BODY ATTACK The Shovel Hook
▾

A shovel hook sits between a hook and an uppercut — a short, compact punch thrown at roughly a 45° angle into the body or chin. It's most effective as a surprise, landed after the opponent's eyes have been pulled upstairs by a jab and cross. Set it up right and it's one of the cleanest ways to finish a round.

FEET

  • Bend the knees slightly to drop your level before throwing, then drive upward through the legs as you release — power comes from underneath, not from reaching down with the arm.
  • Rear foot pivots slightly inward as you throw, same as a hook.

HANDS

  • Set it up behind a jab-cross — by the third punch their eyes are upstairs, not guarding the body.
  • Throw at a 45° angle, scooping upward and across at once. Aim for the liver, or the chin if the body's too well covered.

HILMER KENTY · KRONK ORIGINAL The Flurry Jab
▾

Hilmer Kenty was the first world champion to come out of the Kronk gym, and he got there on a jab thrown in fast, repeated bursts rather than single shots — overwhelming opponents with volume and rhythm before they could find their own range.

FEET

  • Stay light and bouncy rather than planted — this is a volume technique, your feet need to keep resetting between jabs.
  • Small forward steps with each jab in the burst, not one big step. Rapid small adjustments, not a lunge.

HANDS

  • Throw 3–4 jabs in a fast burst rather than one at a time — the first finds range, the rest overwhelm.
  • Keep every jab full speed. Rear hand never drops during the flurry, even though it's tempting to load it for a finisher.

KRONK COUNTER · TRICK The Pull-Counter Right
▾

Rather than blocking or slipping an incoming jab, the pull-counter uses a slight backward lean from the waist to let the punch fall just short — then answers immediately with a straight right while the opponent is still extended and out of position to defend.

FEET

  • Weight shifts onto the back foot as you pull your head back, but keep both feet on the ground — this is a lean, not a retreat.
  • Stay ready to drive forward off the back foot the instant you counter; the pull only works if the answer comes back fast.

HANDS

  • Pull back just enough for the jab to miss by an inch or two — too far costs you the range to counter effectively.
  • Fire the straight right the moment their arm is fully extended. Lead hand stays up near your face the whole time.

FOLDER 2 — SUGAR RAY

Sugar Ray Leonard

Five-Division World Champion · Boxer-Puncher · One of the Four Kings

Sugar Ray Leonard built a career on a simple principle: he never let a fight stay one thing. Against Roberto Duran the first time, he tried to box and got bullied. In the rematch, he danced and clowned and made Duran quit. Against Thomas Hearns, he fell behind on the cards through twelve rounds of being outboxed — then in round thirteen Angelo Dundee told him he was blowing it, and Leonard walked through Hearns in thirteen and stopped him in fourteen. Against Marvin Hagler, he moved for eleven rounds, traded for one, then moved again. Every time the game changed against him, he had another gear. That adaptability is the whole Leonard curriculum.

Underneath the showmanship — the bolo punch, the Ali Shuffle, the taunting — was real, hard technique. His jab did more work than any other punch in his arsenal, used at least four different ways depending on what the moment needed. His footwork wasn't just movement; it was his offense, setting angles before a punch ever landed. He would slide in closer with two or three quick movements in his opponent's direction to force a defensive posture — or cause them to fire a shot he was prepared to slip and counter. Everything in this folder connects back to those two things: the jab and the feet that make it work.

BASE STANCE — THE BOXER-PUNCHER

FOOT POSITION — TOP VIEW

GUARD — FRONT VIEW

FEET

  • Orthodox, shoulder-width — but always live. Leonard almost never planted for more than two consecutive punches. His feet reset between every combination.
  • Weight stays close to 50/50, which gives him the ability to spring forward into power mode OR retreat into pure boxing without needing to shift his base first. Duran beat him the first time partly by forcing him to fight flat-footed against the ropes.
  • Knees slightly bent, never locked. His lead knee had a constant, very subtle flex — you can see it in footage. It's what lets him change direction instantly.
  • He always moves his lead foot first — whether stepping in, out, or laterally. The back foot follows, never crosses.

HANDS

  • High guard, both hands near the face, but the lead hand is always in motion — floating, feinting, measuring. Never glued. It becomes the jab, the bolo setup, the body feint.
  • Rear hand locked at the right cheekbone, elbow in tight to the ribs. Stays there until it's time to fire — then it explodes, then it comes straight back. No lingering.
  • Chin is tucked behind the lead shoulder, not just dropped. Watch his fights — his head is almost always slightly angled so the lead shoulder is between his chin and his opponent.
  • Eyes stay up and tracking the center of his opponent's chest, not their hands. Watching hands is a beginner's habit. Chest movement tells you what's coming before the gloves move.

SUGAR RAY — EXACT POSITIONING

JUST PAST THEIR LEAD GLOVE
LEAD FOOT (L)
15–25°

Points just past the opponent's lead glove and shoulder. Think of it as aiming just outside their extended jab when it's fully out.

Tripod contact: big toe, little toe, heel — all three grounded. Weight through the ball but heel connected. The heel lifts only on the pivot.

Rear heel sits slightly OUTSIDE the lead heel line — not directly behind it. This is Leonard's critical detail: the offset creates the stable triangle for the constant lateral resetting.

Heel stays light. Ball loaded. Rear heel rotates up on the cross.

    COACHING CUES
  • "Lead toe points just past their lead glove." Whenever that glove extends to throw a jab, your front foot is already aimed at the space just outside it. This is what makes the jab and the lateral exit flow naturally together.
  • "Check your rear heel — is it outside the lead?" Drop your eyes down between rounds. If both heels are on the same narrow front-to-back line, you'll tip sideways trying to reset laterally. Widen that rear heel out slightly.
  • "50/50 means alive, not parked." Equal weight doesn't mean still weight. Feel both feet equally but feel them as springs — ready to fire in any direction. If you feel parked, you've lost the 50/50.
  • "Foot first, always." Leonard's jab only works because the lead foot steps simultaneously with the punch. If the foot moves after the jab — you're throwing from flat. Foot and fist land at exactly the same moment.

Why Leonard's footwork is hard: The 50/50 weight balance sounds easy but it's deceptive — most people unconsciously load one foot, and then their movement is slow in one direction. Leonard's real challenge is the constant resetting. He almost never stood still for more than two punches. Building that habit takes coordination training first, then strength to sustain it, then the endurance to keep moving at round 8 the same way you moved at round 1.

JAB MASTERCLASS + FOOTWORK

Leonard almost always used the jab as the initiator for any attack — but he varied the level of the target to confuse opponents. Below are the five versions of his jab, each one tied to specific footwork. These aren't just punch variations — the feet are what make each one work. Drill each version separately before mixing them.

JAB 1 OF 5 · FOOTWORK The Step-In Jab
▾

The foundation. Leonard's default jab — thrown as he steps his lead foot slightly forward to close distance, creating momentum that adds snap without needing extra arm strength. Every other jab variation is a modification of this one.

FEET

  • Lead foot steps forward 3–4 inches at the same moment the jab leaves your hand. Not before, not after — together.
  • Back foot immediately follows, sliding up to restore stance width. If your back foot stays behind, your weight gets dumped forward and you're vulnerable.
  • After the jab lands: immediately circle left or reset — never stand directly in front of where the punch landed.

HANDS

  • Lead shoulder rotates forward, fist extends straight from the cheekbone to the target. Thumb turns down at full extension — your palm should face the floor when the punch connects.
  • Snap back to your guard the instant the fist makes contact. The power is in the snap, not a push.
  • Rear hand does not move at all during the jab. It stays at the cheekbone the whole time.

JAB 2 OF 5 · RHYTHM BREAKER The Triple Jab
▾

Leonard didn't throw the jab once and wait — he fired it in fast, back-to-back bursts. Three jabs in a row overwhelm an opponent's guard before they can reset, and the third jab often found a gap the first two created. Used to set up the right hand or a body attack.

FEET

  • First jab: small step in with the lead foot. Second jab: lead foot resets, another tiny advance. Third jab: same — three micro-steps forward, not one big lunge.

JAB 3 OF 5 · ANGLE JAB The Circling Jab
▾

Leonard would launch the body jab usually as he circled opponents — because of his speed, he was able to control the distance in which he fought. The circling jab isn't just a punch thrown while moving — it's a punch whose angle changes because of where your feet are. You jab from a position your opponent didn't expect you to be in.

FEET

  • Short, sharp snap — circling jabs are not looping or wide. They're the same compact punch thrown from a moving base.

JAB 5 OF 5 · DEFENSIVE JAB The Pull-Back Counter Jab
▾

Most fighters think of the jab as an attacking punch. Leonard also used it as a counter — pulling his head back just enough to let an incoming jab miss by an inch, then snapping his own jab back along exactly the same line before the opponent could recover. The incoming punch creates the opening; the pull-back jab fills it.

FEET

  • Weight shifts onto the back foot during the pull — not a step back, just a lean. You need both feet on the floor to fire back immediately.
  • The moment your own jab goes out, weight shifts forward again onto the front foot to add power to the counter.
  • Don't shuffle backward. The pull is from the waist and head only. If your feet move, you've taken yourself out of range to counter.

HANDS

  • Lean back just enough — maybe 3–4 inches — for the incoming punch to miss your chin. Too far and you're out of range to counter. Too little and you eat it.
  • Fire your jab the instant their arm is at full extension. That's when they're most committed and least able to defend. Your jab goes straight to their chin.
  • Rear hand stays at your cheekbone the whole time — don't drop it to help yourself lean back.

JAB TECHNIQUE TIPS — FIX THESE FIRST

  • ⚠ Dropping the rear hand — your rear hand must not move when you jab. If it drops, you're open for a counter right hand. Drill with your rear fist touching your cheek.
  • ⚠ Arm punching — if the power is coming only from your arm and shoulder, the jab has no snap. Your lead shoulder needs to rotate forward on every jab. Feel the push from the ball of your back foot.
  • ⚠ Not snapping back — leaving your arm extended after the jab is called arm-punching and telegraphs your next punch. The jab returns to your face as fast as it left.
  • ⚠ Standing flat after the jab — every jab should end with your feet moving. Either circle left, reset your angle, or set up your next punch while moving. Standing still is a target.
  • ⚠ Only jabbing to the head — Leonard used the body jab constantly to drain energy and pull the guard down. If you only go upstairs, your opponent only guards one place.
  • ⚠ Telegraphing with the shoulder — if your lead shoulder dips or rises before the jab goes out, a sharp opponent will time it. The jab should erupt from a neutral shoulder position.

PHASE 1 — COORDINATION

THE CORRECT FORWARD LEAN

Stand in Duran stance. Lean your whole body forward from the ankles — not from the waist. Your ears, shoulders, hips, and knees should all be on the same angled line. If your back rounds or your butt sticks out, reset. The lean is athletic, not hunched.

Check in mirror daily · 2 min

PRESSURE WALK DRILL

Walk forward slowly in Duran stance — compact steps, weight slightly forward, head moving side to side. Stay in stance the whole walk. Every time your weight drifts back or your posture rounds, stop and reset from scratch.

4 x 20 steps · slow and deliberate

Imagine an opponent circling right. Instead of following straight, cut diagonally to intercept — step your lead foot to the right to close off their escape path. Practice both directions until the diagonal feels natural instead of a conscious decision.

Lie on your back, arms up, legs at 90°. Slowly lower opposite arm and leg together, keeping your lower back flat on the floor. Builds the deep core stability that holds the forward lean clean instead of collapsing into a hunch.

TEACHING KRONK

THE 3 THINGS EVERY KRONK STUDENT GETS WRONG FIRST

  • Dropping to flat feet after the jab — they extend the jab, their weight shifts forward, and they land on their heel. Fix: have them jab and immediately rise back onto the ball of the lead foot. Do it 20 times slow before adding any speed.
  • Pivoting with the weight forward — they try to pivot when their weight has already shifted to the front foot, which makes the pivot slow and off-balance. Fix: the pivot starts BEFORE the weight shift completes — it's mid-punch, not after.
  • The rear hand drifts on the cross — after the cross, their rear hand hangs instead of snapping back. Fix: have them say "snap" out loud as the fist returns. The audio cue trains the habit faster than just telling them.

KRONK COACHING CUES THAT WORK

  • "Reach before you step" — for the jab. Lead hand extends first, foot follows, not the other way around.
  • "Hip to hand" — for the cross. The hip rotation drives the punch. If they're all arm, point to their hip and say this.
  • "Pivot and find them" — after the pivot, they often lose track of where the pads are. Cue: turn and find the target immediately after pivoting, not after a pause.
  • "Tall and quiet" — for stance. If they start squatting or bouncing, remind them: tall posture, quiet feet between punches.

TEACHING SUGAR RAY

THE 3 THINGS EVERY SUGAR RAY STUDENT GETS WRONG FIRST

  • Moving after the punch instead of with it — they throw, then move. The stance reset needs to happen as the last punch lands, not after a pause. Fix: have them take one step while throwing the last punch of every combo.
  • Throwing feints with the whole body — a big wind-up feint is easy to read. Leonard's feints were small — a shoulder roll, a partial extension, a knee dip. Fix: have them practice a feint that moves only the shoulder. If their feet move, it's too big.
  • Losing the 50/50 weight balance under pressure — when they get hit or pressured, they load back onto the rear foot and get stuck. Fix: teach them to reset the weight balance immediately after eating a punch — it's a trained response, not a natural one.

SUGAR RAY COACHING CUES THAT WORK

  • "Jab is not a stop sign" — every jab ends with movement. Say this after every jab they throw while standing still.
  • "Circle to survive" — after being pressured. Don't back up in a straight line, circle to safety.
  • "Small feints, big reactions" — the point of the feint is to get a reaction, not to look dramatic. Smaller feints are actually harder for an opponent to ignore.
  • "Find the chest" — when they're watching hands instead of the center of mass. Chest movement predicts punches. Hands react to punches.

TEACHING DURAN

THE 3 THINGS EVERY DURAN STUDENT GETS WRONG FIRST

  • Hunching the back on the forward lean — they try to lean forward but it comes from rounding the back, not from the ankles. This destroys their power base. Fix: have them lean forward like a wall that's tilting — the whole body on one angle, back straight, chin tucked.
  • Retreating after body shots — Duran never backs up after landing a body shot. Students instinctively step back after punching. Fix: the footwork drill is: punch, then immediately shuffle FORWARD one step. Make the forward step part of the habit, not optional.
  • Losing head movement as they get tired — by round 3–4, the head stops moving and they just plod forward. This is a conditioning issue but also a focus issue. Fix: in training, call "head" periodically — they must dip or move their head on that cue. Keeps it in the foreground.

DURAN COACHING CUES THAT WORK

  • "Lean from the ankles" — not the waist, not the neck. Whole body tilted forward as one unit. Tap their ankles if they're bending at the waist.
  • "Stay on them" — after every punch. Don't back up. Don't reset to distance. Stay in their face.
  • "Head moving, always" — especially when pressing forward. If their head stops, that's a target. Cue this every round.
  • "Compact steps" — when they start lunging. Duran's footwork is short, controlled advances. Long steps overcommit and cost balance.

TONEY FOUNDATION · THE GUARDThe Philly Shell Guard
▾

The stance involves keeping the lead hand draped across the body to block body shots while using the shoulder roll, head movement, and rear hand to defend punches to the head. A bladed stance makes the target smaller. This is the foundation every other Toney technique is built on. Get this wrong and nothing else works.

SETUP

  • Lead arm hangs LOW and ACROSS the body — not in front of the face. It protects the ribs and body from hooks.
  • Lead shoulder RAISED to approximately chin level — creates the "shell" barrier that deflects punches heading for the head.
  • Elevating the lead shoulder slightly creates a defensive shell that protects critical bodily regions.

HANDS

  • Rear hand sits at the chin — actively protecting the most important target. This is the last line of defence when the shell doesn't deflect enough.
  • Both hands are simultaneously ready to counter — the rear hand fires from its chin position, the lead hand whips out from the body to jab or hook.
  • Note: Pick up your lead hand to protect against punches to your head when things get heated inside the ring. There's nothing wrong with a conventional high guard when needed.

TONEY SIGNATURE · DEFLECT & COUNTERThe Shoulder Roll
▾

Boxers using the Philly Shell lean back slightly, raising the front shoulder to deflect incoming punches — as if to say "nice try" without uttering a word. This smooth defensive move is all about finesse and timing. The shoulder roll is not a block — it's a DEFLECTION. The punch rides up and over the raised shoulder instead of landing clean.

MECHANICS

  • Incoming jab or straight left: rotate lead shoulder FORWARD and slightly up — the punch rides over the shoulder and misses.
  • The rotation is a small, efficient movement — not a dramatic dip. Just enough to redirect the punch's path.
  • The body lean-back accompanies the roll — a slight weight shift to the rear foot adds distance while the shoulder does the work.

THE COUNTER

  • The counter fires immediately off the roll — the rear hand is already loaded at the chin from the shell position and fires as the shoulder comes through.
  • From the defensive posture, the boxer quickly uncorks a lead right hand. This counter can catch an opponent off-guard and shift the momentum of the bout.
  • Most common Toney counters off the roll: right hand over the top, left hook to the body, jab-cross as they reset.

TONEY SYSTEM · CONTROLLED AGGRESSIONThe Pressure Game — Defensive Aggression
▾

Constant grind forward. Rarely took a backward step. Turn the screw. Defensively-minded aggression. The paradox that defined Toney: he was one of the best defensive boxers of all time AND one of the most relentlessly forward-moving fighters. The shell allowed him to press forward without taking the damage that forward-moving fighters normally absorb.

THE CONCEPT

  • Move forward with the shell active. The shell is your protection while advancing. You don't wait for the opponent to come to you — you bring the shell to them.
  • The closer you get, the more effective both the shell AND the counter become. At close range, the lead arm blocks body shots automatically. At range, it's less effective.
  • Forward pressure forces opponents to throw. When they throw → you roll → you counter. The pressure creates the opportunities.

EXECUTION

  • Small, controlled steps forward. Not lunging — grinding. Every step forward is a deliberate taking of space.
  • When the opponent backs up: don't rush. Keep the same controlled pace. Closing distance slowly is more dangerous than rushing in.
  • If hit on the way in: don't panic, don't retreat. Roll it, keep the position you've earned, counter from there. Giving up position after eating a shot is the biggest beginner mistake.

TONEY CLOSE RANGE · BODY ATTACKInside Fighting + Body Work
▾

Toney was an exceptional inside fighter who often preferred to fight off the ropes. Toney seamlessly flowed from defense to attack, utilizing elusive in-fighting and devastating counter shots to outwit his adversaries. At close range, the shell's lead arm automatically covers the body from hooks. This lets him focus entirely on landing body work without worrying about incoming body shots.

POSITION

  • Head position at close range: head on the opponent's chest or shoulder side — never directly in front of them. This makes it harder for them to land hooks.
  • Knees bent, lower than the opponent, punching UP into their body. This angle is more damaging and harder to defend against.
  • Lead arm: at close range, the lead arm moves from body-draped position to actively posting and controlling — pushing their arm away to open the body.

PUNCHES

  • Short left hook to the liver: 6-inch punch, bent elbow, rapid hip snap. This is Toney's most dangerous close-range punch.
  • Short uppercut to body: dig up from below, catch the ribs or solar plexus. Fire this when they expect the hook.
  • Right hand over the top: when they tuck their chin down to protect the body — fire the right hand over the top to the head. Body sets up the head.

TONEY SPECIALTY · ROPE CONTROLFighting Off the Ropes
▾

Most fighters end up on the ropes by accident. Toney went there deliberately. The ropes behind him meant he had a solid backing to lean into, which enhanced the shoulder roll's effectiveness. With his back near the ropes, he could absorb the opponent's energy, make them throw, roll everything, and counter without needing to circle away.

ON THE ROPES

  • Don't get trapped — choose to be there. There's a difference between being pushed to the ropes and walking yourself there to work.
  • Use the ropes as a backboard. Lean slightly into them, let them support the lean-back on rolls. The energy you don't spend staying upright goes into faster counters.
  • Head movement is constant on the ropes — never a still target. Slip, roll, lean — the ropes don't make you stationary.

GETTING OUT

  • Exit the ropes the moment you've landed your counter. Don't stay there after scoring. Roll off to the side — left or right — and reset to the center.
  • Exit with a punch: as you move off the ropes, throw a punch in the direction of your exit. This stops them from following immediately.
  • Never exit by going forward into the opponent — always to the side. The side exit keeps you in counter range without walking into their next punch.

TONEY FINISHER · KO WEAPONThe Overhand Right Counter
▾

An overhand right can get past an opponent's lead shoulder and connect with their temple when they're using the Philly Shell — but Toney used the same punch as a counter, firing it over the top of the incoming jab just as his shoulder finished rolling. This is Toney's most dangerous punch when the shell is working: the opponent commits to a jab, the shoulder deflects it, and the overhand right arrives at the temple before they can pull back.

THE SETUP

  • The shell invites jabs — the opponent sees an open face (because the lead hand is low) and fires a jab. This is the trap.
  • As the jab hits the raised shoulder and deflects: the rear hand is already loading from the chin position. It fires in a slight overhand arc — coming OVER the opponent's extended arm and landing on the temple.
  • The timing: the overhand fires as your shoulder is completing its roll. You're counter-punching into the brief moment when their arm is fully extended and they're most vulnerable.

THE PUNCH

  • Not a looping haymaker — a controlled overhand. The arc is enough to come over their lead arm, not a full circular swing.
  • Fire from the chin position: the rear hand doesn't drop or pull back first. It fires directly from its guard position in a compact overhand arc.
  • Follow immediately with the left hook or step to the side. Don't stand in front after landing — the opponent's next punch is already coming.
